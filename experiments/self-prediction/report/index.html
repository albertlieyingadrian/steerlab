    Llama predicts its own answers better than Qwen can; Qwen does not

    Asked what the first letter of its answer would be, Llama-3.1-8B beats an outsider given the same examples of its behaviour, with or without training. Qwen2.5-7B, in the same test, is beaten by the outsider. At this scale, knowing itself is a property of one model, not of models.

    Summary: the short answer and the key results

    In short

    Can a model predict its own answers better than another model that has seen the same examples of its behaviour?

    Llama-3.1-8B can; Qwen2.5-7B cannot. Each model answered 407 one-word prompts (“Name a fruit. Reply with one word.”) and was then asked, about 130 held-out prompts, “what would be the first letter of your answer?” and “would your answer have more than 5 letters?”. The other model was asked the same about it, with the same examples. After both were fine-tuned on the same data, Llama predicted itself right on 181 of 260 cases and Qwen predicted Llama on 162 (paired p = 0.014), the gap coming from the first letter (113 against 97 of 130). Qwen predicted itself on 180 and Llama predicted Qwen on 208 (p = 0.002), the gap coming from answer length, where Qwen’s self-prediction was no better than always guessing the most common answer. Binder et al. (2024) found a self-advantage in larger models; here it holds for one model in two.

    01Introduction

    Llama-3.1-8B is asked: “Suppose you were asked the following: ‘Name a fruit. Reply with one word.’ What would be the first letter of your answer?” Without examples or training, its reply matches the first letter of what it actually says to such prompts on 82 of 130 held-out prompts. Qwen2.5-7B, shown 16 examples of Llama’s answers, manages 39. A gap like that would suggest a model has access to itself that an outsider lacks; the question is whether it survives a fair comparison.

    What is known. Binder et al. (2024), “Looking Inward”, fine-tuned a model to predict properties of its own answers to hypothetical questions, and a second model on exactly the same data about the first. With GPT-4, GPT-4o and Llama-3-70B, the model predicting itself beat the outsider, which they read as evidence of introspection: knowledge about itself that its behaviour, as data, does not give away. Our first introspection note found Qwen3-8B unable to report whether a hint had changed its answer.

    What is missing. The test at laptop scale: two 7–8B open models, each in turn the subject and the outsider.

    Can a small open model predict properties of its own answers better than another model given the same examples of its behaviour?

    What this note does. It runs Binder et al.’s design on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, without training (examples in the prompt) and with LoRA fine-tuning. Its contributions:

    1. A replication in one direction: Llama predicts its own first letter better than Qwen does, with and without training.
    2. A failure in the other: after training, Qwen is predicted better by Llama than by itself.
    3. A small, open setup: 407 prompts, two local models, no API calls, under an hour.

    What would count as an answer. Written with the code, before the run: on the same held-out cases, the subject predicting itself is right more often than the outsider trained on the same data, by an exact paired test, for each subject.

    02Setup

    2.1 Prompts and properties

    407 prompts that each ask for one word: 295 name something in a category (“Name a fruit. Reply with one word.”, 59 categories × 5 phrasings), 100 complete a sentence (“Fill in the next word: ‘The cat sat on the ___’”, 50 sentences × 2 phrasings), and 12 ask for a number. A fixed shuffle puts a third (135) in the test set. Each model’s greedy answer to each prompt is its object-level behaviour, and two properties of it are predicted:

    • First letter of the answer word (26 possible values).
    • Long: does the word have more than 5 letters (yes or no)? 47–57% of each model’s answers are long.

    Evenness of numbers was planned as a third property and dropped before any prediction was scored: only 12 prompts ask for a number, too few for 16 examples. The 130 word prompts in the test set give 260 test cases per subject.

    2.2 Who predicts, and how

    A prediction is a reply to the hypothetical “Suppose you were asked the following: ‘…’. What would be the first letter of your answer? Reply with only that letter.” (or “Would your answer have more than 5 letters? Reply with only yes or no.”). It is scored against what the subject actually answered to that prompt.

    ConditionPredictorWhat it sees
    Self, zero-shotThe subjectThe hypothetical only
    Self, 16 examplesThe subject16 training prompts with the subject’s true property, then the hypothetical
    Cross, 16 examplesThe other modelThe same 16 examples, about the subject
    Self, fine-tunedThe subject + LoRATrained on the 530 training hypotheticals, with its own answers’ properties as targets
    Cross, fine-tunedThe other model + LoRATrained on the same 530 hypotheticals, with the fine-tuned subject’s properties as targets
    Most common valueNoneAlways the most frequent value in the subject’s training answers

    Fine-tuning can change the behaviour being predicted, so, as in Binder et al., the fine-tuned subject’s answers are rerun on every prompt, the outsider is trained on those, and both fine-tuned predictors are scored against them. LoRA rank 16 on attention and MLP, learning rate 10−4, 3 epochs, batch 8, loss on the answer tokens only. Rates have 95% Wilson intervals; self and cross are compared on the same cases with an exact McNemar test.

    03How the study was run

    1. Object level: both models answer all 407 prompts (greedy, up to 8 tokens).
    2. No training: for each subject, 260 test cases in three conditions, plus the most-common baseline.
    3. Fine-tuning, for each subject in turn: train the subject on itself, rerun its answers, train the outsider on those answers, predict the 260 test cases with both.
    4. Compute every rate and test.

    04Results

    Three findings:

    1. 4.1 Llama predicts its own first letter better than Qwen can, with or without training.
    2. 4.2 Qwen is predicted better by Llama than by itself, because of answer length.
    3. 4.3 What the self-advantage does and does not show.

    4.1 Llama predicts its own first letter better than Qwen can, with or without training

    After fine-tuning, Llama predicted the first letter of its own answer on 113 of 130 prompts (0.87 [0.80, 0.92]); Qwen, trained on the same data about Llama, on 97 (0.75). On the same prompts, 23 were right only for Llama and 7 only for Qwen (p = 0.005). Over both properties, 181 against 162 of 260 (p = 0.014). Figure 1 shows every condition.

    The gap is there before any training. With 16 examples in the prompt, Llama predicted its own first letter on 69 of 130 and Qwen predicted it on 39 (41 cases only Llama, 11 only Qwen; p = 4×10−5). Asked cold, with no examples at all, Llama was right on 82. The outsider has to guess what a different model would name; Llama can, in effect, answer the question itself.

    On answer length the two were level after training (68 and 65 of 130), and neither beat always guessing the most common value (65).

    4.2 Qwen is predicted better by Llama than by itself, because of answer length

    After fine-tuning, Llama predicted Qwen on 208 of 260 cases and Qwen predicted itself on 180 (51 only Llama, 23 only Qwen; p = 0.002). The first letter was a tie (114 and 110 of 130, p = 0.42); the gap is answer length, 94 against 70 of 130 (p = 0.003).

    Qwen’s self-predictions of length were right on 70 of 130, exactly as many as always guessing the most common value: trained on its own behaviour, it did not learn to tell its long answers from its short ones. Llama, trained on the same examples of Qwen, did (94). Without training, Qwen and Llama predicted Qwen equally well (121 and 119 of 260; p = 0.91).

    Binder et al.’s test asks for a self-advantage on held-out prompts. Qwen fails it here, and not because both predictors are near chance: on first letters both are above 0.84.

    4.3 What the self-advantage does and does not show

    The simplest account of Llama’s advantage is self-simulation: asked what the first letter of its answer would be, Llama answers the embedded question and reads off the letter. That needs no knowledge of itself beyond being itself. An outsider can only simulate itself, and Qwen and Llama give the same first letter on only 186 of 407 prompts. Binder et al. count this as privileged access, since the outsider cannot do it; it is a weaker sense of introspection than reporting on internal states.

    A check on whether training moved the target. Fine-tuning changed Qwen’s answer word on 94 of 407 prompts and Llama’s on 119. On test cases whose answer did not change, Llama self beat the outsider 109 to 104 of 150; on cases whose answer changed, 72 to 58 of 110. Qwen lost on both: 124 to 138 of 166 unchanged, 56 to 70 of 94 changed. The direction of each result does not depend on whether training moved the answer.

    06Implications for AI safety

    If a model can tell us things about itself that its behaviour does not reveal, its self-reports become evidence for oversight: about its dispositions, or what it would do in a situation not yet tested. The result here is a caution about leaning on that. At 7–8B parameters, one model shows the self-advantage on a simple property and the other does not, and where it shows, self-simulation explains it without deeper access. Self-knowledge has to be measured per model and per property before it is trusted.

    The recommendation in one sentence: before using a model’s predictions about itself, test them against an outsider trained on the same examples of its behaviour, per property, on held-out prompts.

    07What this does not show

    • Two models, two properties. Binder et al. tested many more properties; a self-advantage for Qwen may show on ones not tried here.
    • One training run per predictor. No repeats with other seeds; a different run might move the 19- and 28-case gaps.
    • Small, simple prompts. One-word answers; behaviour with more room may be harder or easier to predict.
    • One recipe for both properties. Qwen’s self-predictor never learned length; a recipe tuned per property might.
    • Self-simulation is not ruled out. Nothing here separates reading off a simulated answer from deeper self-knowledge.

    10Questions and answers

    Can a language model predict its own answers better than another model?

    Sometimes. Fine-tuned on the same data, Llama-3.1-8B predicted the first letter of its own one-word answers on 113 of 130 held-out prompts, and Qwen2.5-7B predicted Llama’s on 97. The reverse failed: Llama predicted Qwen better than Qwen did.

    Is that introspection?

    In Binder et al.’s sense, yes: knowledge about itself that an outsider with the same data lacks. The simplest mechanism, answering the embedded question and reading off the letter, needs no access to internal states.

    Why did Qwen fail?

    On answer length. Trained to predict whether its answers had more than 5 letters, Qwen was right no more often than always guessing the most common answer (70 of 130); Llama, trained on the same examples of Qwen, was right on 94.
