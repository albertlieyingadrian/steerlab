    What does it mean to formalise a domain? Airline bag rules, a checker and its proofs

    With the rules pasted in, Qwen3-8B gets 82 of 200 held-out baggage questions exactly right. Checking each step of its answer against the same rules, written as code and traced to their source lines, gets 164. Most wrong answers start from citing the wrong rule, not from bad arithmetic.

    Summary: the short answer and the key results

    In short

    What changes when a rulebook is formalised, and an open model's answers are checked against it?

    A slice of RuleArena's airline-baggage rules (158 lines) was turned into 162 machine-checkable rules, each traced to its line, and Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B answered 200 held-out questions three ways. With no rules they almost never got the exact total (11 and 21 of 200, no better than quoting the ticket price). With the rules pasted in they got 82 and 117. When a checker built from the formal rules verified each step of their answers and recomputed what failed, they got 164 and 191, and every answer came with a proof: the rule used at each step, its inputs, and where the model went wrong. Nearly every wrong answer cited at least one wrong rule; wrong arithmetic with the right rules was rare. What the checker could not fix were the facts the model handed it, mostly a carry-on priced as a checked bag. A design-system rulebook (GOV.UK colour and WCAG contrast rules) showed the same pattern.

    01Introduction

    Daniel flies Premium Economy from Chicago to Berlin with a backpack and three checked bags, two of them far over the size limit. Qwen3-8B, with every line of the airline's baggage rules pasted into its prompt, charged $200 for each of those two: the fee in line 361, for flights within the U.S. and Canada. For Europe, line 367 says $150. A checker built from the same lines, rewritten as code, marked those two steps as failed, named the line the model should have used, and returned $1,177 instead of $1,277.

    What is known. RuleArena (ACL 2025) asked language models to apply three real rulebooks (airline baggage fees, NBA transactions, U.S. income tax) and found them weak at it: they pick the wrong rule, and miscompute even when they pick the right one. One response is to formalise the domain: turn the rulebook into rules a program can check, each traced to its source, and have the program return a proof a person can inspect instead of an answer they have to trust.

    What is missing. "Formalise the domain" is easy to say and hard to picture. What does a formal rule look like next to the sentence it came from? Which rules does a rulebook leave unstated? How much does checking a model's answer against formal rules add over pasting the rules into its prompt, and which of the model's mistakes does it catch?

    What does formalising a small rulebook take, and how does an open model do alone, with the rules pasted in, and with its answers checked against the formal rules?

    What this note does. It formalises a slice of RuleArena's airline rules by hand and runs the three arms on held-out questions with exact answers. It contributes:

    1. a formal spec of the slice: 162 rules, each with its kind, conditions, outcome and source line, and the four rules the text does not settle;
    2. a checker that returns a proof artifact for every answer, checked against RuleArena's own answer code on 5,000 random passengers;
    3. a measurement of rule identification apart from rule application, from the line each answer cites for each fee;
    4. the same treatment of a design-system rulebook (colour and contrast), as a bridge to brand rules;
    5. an interactive proof explorer: change a bag and watch the rules and the proof update (section 5.6).

    What counts as success. Written down before any run:

    1. the checker agrees with RuleArena's own answer code on every random passenger (otherwise the formalisation is wrong);
    2. pasting the rules beats no rules and the cheap baselines, and asking for a trace does not by itself change accuracy;
    3. the checker beats the pasted rules on the same replies, and never passes a wrong answer;
    4. the same holds on a second, unrelated rulebook.

    02What formalising a rule means

    A rulebook written for people is a list of sentences. Formalising it means rewriting each sentence as a rule a program can apply, without losing track of where it came from. Each formal rule here has five parts:

    • A kind: a definition (what "size" means), a condition (a fee that applies when its conditions hold), an exception (a rule that overrides another for some cases) or a combination (how two fees add up).
    • When it applies: the fare class, the route, the direction of travel, which checked bag it is (first, second, third), and the weight or size band.
    • What it decides: a fee, an allowance, or "not accepted".
    • Its source: the line number in the rulebook and the line's exact words.
    • What it overrides, if it is an exception.

    Take one passenger: Business class from Seattle to Tokyo, whose first checked bag weighs 62 lb and measures 64 inches (length + width + height). Figure 1 follows one rule through the three forms it takes.

    A proof artifact is the list of such steps for a whole question: every rule used, the inputs it read and the value it decided, and, when it checks someone else's answer, the first step that failed. It replaces a number nobody can audit with a record a person can read line by line, the way a reviewer checks a tax return.

    What the formaliser had to decide. The slice (six of RuleArena's 21 regions, all six fare classes, one to three checked bags) became 162 rules from 158 source lines: 150 conditions, 3 definitions, 4 exceptions, 1 combination rule, and 4 rules the text does not settle (Table 1).

    Table 1: the four rules the text does not settle

The spec follows the section headings. A lint step checks that every input the slice allows is decided by exactly one fee rule per component (no gaps, no overlaps), and the checker built on the spec agrees with RuleArena's own answer code on 5,000 of 5,000 random passengers (section 3).

    03Setup

    The questions. RuleArena's own generator, with three changes. Only the slice's six regions (U.S. domestic, Mexico, Cuba, Europe, Japan, Australia), picked evenly. One to three checked bags plus a carry-on (RuleArena's easiest level has four checked bags). And each checked bag is overweight with probability 0.6 and oversize with probability 0.6, independently: RuleArena makes every bag both, so its questions never test the allowance rules. A question reads like this:

    David is a Basic Economy Class passenger flying from Minneapolis to Los Angeles with the following items:
1. A backpack: 18 x 12 x 7 inches, 11 lbs;
2. A backpack: 37 x 16 x 9 inches, 31 lbs;
3. A luggage box: 35 x 15 x 13 inches, 52 lbs;

David's flight ticket is $100.

    The model must find the carry-on (item 1 fits the 22 × 14 × 9 inch sizer), price the two checked bags and add the ticket. The answer, $215, comes from RuleArena's own answer code, unchanged. Questions and answers were generated and their hashes recorded before any model replied (data/manifest.json).

    Three arms, and a twin.

    • (a) LLM alone. The question and RuleArena's instruction ("Compute the total cost … step by step … end your response with 'The total cost is $xxx.'"), with no rules: whatever the model already knows about a U.S. airline's 2024 bag fees.
    • (b) LLM with the rules pasted. The slice's 187 source lines, word for word, each labelled with its line number (baggage_rules_slice.txt), then the question. After its working the model writes a trace: the facts it read (fare class, region, direction, ticket, each checked bag's size and weight) and, for each checked bag, the line it used for the base fee, the weight fee and the size fee, each fee, the bag's total and the grand total.
    • (b0) the free-answer twin. The same rules and RuleArena's own instruction, with no trace. It shows whether asking for a trace changes the answers.
    • (c) LLM proposes, checker verifies. Not a new prompt: the checker reads arm (b)'s trace, evaluates the spec on the facts the model wrote down, and compares every step. If every step passes, the model's answer stands, with its proof. If any fails, the proof records where, and the checker's own total, computed from the model's facts, replaces the answer. So (b) and (c) are scored on the same replies, question by question.

    Rule identification and rule application are scored apart, on arm (b)'s traces. Identification: for each checked bag and each of its three fees, did the model cite the line the spec uses, given the true facts? Strict means exactly that line; loose also accepts a line within two of it, or a line stating the same fee (a citation slip rather than a wrong rule). Application: with the right lines in hand, did the model compute right? Every wrong answer gets one label, its first failure, checked in this order: no usable trace, a fact misread, a wrong rule cited (strict), and otherwise application.

    Models and settings. Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B (FP8 weights), thinking on, at Qwen's recommended thinking settings (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, top-k 20), up to 12,000 new tokens, one reply per question, served with vLLM 0.10.1.1 on one H100. A second reply to every held-out question (seed 1) from Qwen3-8B in arm (b) measures sampling noise. The system prompt is RuleArena's, with the airline's name replaced by "an airline".

    Baselines. Three answers that need no model: the ticket price alone; the ticket plus the U.S. Main Cabin fees for every passenger (no exceptions); and the checker alone, reading the facts from the question with a regular expression and mapping cities to regions with RuleArena's city table.

    04How the study was run

    1. Write the plan with its design and four predictions, and commit it before any model run (the predictions are in section 7).
    2. Formalise the slice: parse the fee lines, map each line's route wording to the six regions by hand, write the prose rules and the four unsettled ones, and lint the result (162 rules, no gaps or overlaps).
    3. Check every ruler against a bar set in the plan: the checker against RuleArena's answer code (5,000/5,000), the JavaScript port behind the interactive against the Python checker (5,000/5,000, every step's rules too), the reply parser on 20 hand-written replies (20/20), and the contrast formula on 10 published colour pairs (10/10). All passed (ruler.json).
    4. Generate and freeze the questions and answers (hashes in the manifest).
    5. Tune on dev only: one run of Qwen3-8B on the 40 dev questions and 30 design questions, then four changes to the parser and scorer, each logged in results.tsv. The prompts were not changed.
    6. Run held-out once: both models, every arm, plus the second Qwen3-8B sample.
    7. Score with a 95% bootstrap over questions (10,000 resamples), and paired bootstraps for differences between arms on the same questions.

    05Results

    5.1 Pasting the rules helps; checking against the formal rules helps more

    Figure 2 shows the exact-answer rate of every arm. Without the rules, both models almost never land on the exact total: 11 and 21 of 200, no better than answering with the ticket price alone, which is right for the 21 questions whose bags are all free and within limits. Their replies guess at a typical U.S. airline's fees and often name one.

    With the slice's rules pasted in, Qwen3-8B gets 82 of 200 and Qwen3-32B 117, gains of +0.355 [0.285, 0.425] and +0.48 [0.405, 0.555] over no rules on the same questions. The checker arm, run on the same replies, gets 164 and 191: +0.41 [0.335, 0.48] and +0.37 [0.30, 0.44] over the pasted rules. It never passed a wrong answer. It turned 85 and 76 wrong answers right and 3 and 2 right answers wrong (section 5.4).

    The boring explanations. Four checks, each against an explanation that would make these numbers mean less:

    • Asking for a trace changes the answers. The free-answer twin (b0) scored 91 and 125, level with the traced arm: differences −0.045 [−0.13, 0.045] and −0.04 [−0.125, 0.045]. The trace costs nothing measurable and gives the checker something to read.
    • One sample is luck. A second sample of every Qwen3-8B reply (seed 1) scored 84 with the rules and 159 with the checker; the paired difference from the first is −0.01 [−0.09, 0.075].
    • Replies ran out of tokens. At most one reply per model and arm hit the 12,000-token cap. Leaving them out moves no rate by more than 0.005.
    • The checker is just doing the arithmetic. Partly true, and the regex baseline says how much: reading the facts from the question with a regular expression and running the checker gets 200 of 200. The checker arm falls short of that only where it trusted the facts the model wrote down (section 5.4).

    5.2 Wrong answers start with a wrong rule, not with wrong arithmetic

    Each traced answer names, for every checked bag, the line it used for the base fee, the weight fee and the size fee. Comparing those citations with the lines the spec uses separates finding the rule from applying it. Figure 3 gives the first thing that went wrong in each wrong answer.

    • Almost every wrong answer cites a wrong rule somewhere: 116 of 117 wrong answers with a usable trace (Qwen3-8B) and 81 of 82 (Qwen3-32B). As the first failure, a wrong rule accounts for 76 of 117 and 72 of 82; facts account for most of the rest.
    • Right rules with wrong arithmetic is rare: 0 and 1 answers strictly. Counting near-miss citations as right (a line within two of the spec's, or one stating the same fee), it rises to 7 and 15, still a small share.
    • Citations are sloppy even in right answers. Across all bags, the cited line is exactly the spec's for 473 of 1,137 fees (0.42) and 644 of 1,140 (0.56); loosely, 0.73 and 0.83. On traces whose facts were right, so that wrong positions cannot drag citations along, the strict rates are 0.45 and 0.56. A random line of the right section would be exact 4.8% of the time. The weight fee is cited worst (0.23 and 0.38 strict): the models point at the size line beside the weight line, at a section heading, or at the ordinary 50 lb allowance when an exception (Business, First, Australia) applies.
    • The exceptions are where it goes wrong. An exception (the Business/First or Australia allowance, or the Main Plus footnote) appears in the spec's proof of 118 of the 200 questions. The rule the text never states, which bag counts as the first checked bag, changes the answer in 5 of 200.

    5.4 The checker cannot fix the facts it is handed

    The checker arm evaluates the spec on the facts the model wrote into its trace. When those facts are wrong, the checker computes the right fees for the wrong passenger. Qwen3-8B's 36 remaining errors with the checker break down as:

    • 25 from listing the carry-on (item 1) as a checked bag: 13 where the checker priced it, and 12 where the extra bag took the trip past the slice (a fourth checked bag, or a third to Cuba) and the checker could not evaluate it, so the model's own answer stood;
    • 4 more outside the slice for other reasons, 3 wrong regions, 1 wrong direction for Cuba, 2 other bag lists, and 1 reply with no trace.

    Qwen3-32B's 9: 4 carry-ons priced as checked, 3 wrong directions for Cuba, 1 bag list and 1 missing trace. The 3 and 2 answers the checker turned wrong have the same cause: a wrong fact in the trace (the carry-on, a wrong region, a wrong direction for Cuba) that the model's own working had not used, so its total was right and the checker's, built on the trace, was not.

    The carry-on rule is a definition (line 20: a carry-on fits 22 × 14 × 9 inches). The trace asked the model for each bag's total size, not its three dimensions, so the model applied that definition and handed the checker its conclusion. The regex baseline, which reads the dimensions, makes no such error. The lesson for the pipeline is to hand the checker raw facts, and let it apply every rule it can.

    5.5 A design rulebook: the same pattern, sharper

    The bridge to brand rules uses the GOV.UK Design System's colour page and the WCAG 2.2 lines it points to: the palette rule ("Always use the GOV.UK colour palette"), the focus colour rule ("Only use this colour to indicate which element is focused on"), and contrast at 4.5:1 for text, 3:1 for large text, with exemptions for inactive controls and logos. Formalised, it is 12 rules: 10 traced to source lines (source.txt) and 2 interpretations the text needs, points converted to CSS pixels (WCAG gives points; 18 pt is 24 px) and which elements count as exempt. Each question gives an element, its size and weight, and two colours (120 held-out; 52 compliant).

    • Without the rules the verdict is a coin toss: 67 and 65 of 120, level with always answering "not compliant" (68).
    • With the rules, the verdict improves and the reasons do not: 82 and 95 verdicts right, but the verdict and the broken rules both right for only 33 and 42 (+0.125 [0.017, 0.233] and +0.25 [0.142, 0.358] over no rules on the verdict). The models named the right contrast rule for 90 and 77 elements, and computed the contrast ratio within 0.05 for only 43 and 26: the luminance formula raises each colour channel to the power 2.4, which is hard to do in one's head. Near the thresholds (within 1.0 of 4.5 or 3), verdicts were right 31 and 33 times out of 51.
    • The checker gets all 120 right for both models: every model copied the colours, size and weight into its trace correctly (after the dev fix in Table 3), so nothing was left for the checker to inherit.
    • A note on strictness. Many replies listed D3, the line requiring WCAG 1.4.3, beside the contrast line that failed. The pre-registered score counts that as a mismatch. Read as naming the same rule (an exploratory reading added after seeing the replies), the pasted arm gets 57 of 120 for both models; the checker's lead stays large either way.

    The checker runs in this page, on the same spec (a JavaScript port that agrees with the Python checker on 5,000 of 5,000 random passengers, every step's rules included).     06Implications for AI safety

    Systems that must follow rules with consequences (refund policies, compliance checks, content rules, a brand's guidelines) are often built by pasting the rules into a model's prompt. On a rulebook small enough to fit, two open models with the rules in hand still got 41% and 59% of answers exactly right, and nearly every wrong answer started with the wrong rule. Reading the rules is not the same as finding the one that applies, least of all an exception a few lines further down.

    Formalising the rules changes what can be checked. The checker never passed a wrong answer, and each failure came with the rule and the step. Its limits are clear too. It inherits whatever facts it is given, so the boundary between what the model reads and what the checker decides should sit as close to the raw input as possible. And the formalisation has to be right first: four of 162 rules here had to be decided by someone, one from code the rulebook's readers never see.

    Recommendation: where a rule can be written as code, check the model's answer against it and keep the proof; ask the model for raw facts, not conclusions; and measure whether it finds the right rule separately from whether it computes correctly.

    08What this does not show

    • One small slice, by one formaliser. Six regions, one to three checked bags, formalised by hand. RuleArena's harder levels (four to ten bags), its other regions, and its tax and NBA rulebooks were not run. A model or another person might formalise the same lines differently.
    • Generated questions. The questions follow RuleArena's generator, with clean, explicit facts. Real requests are messier, which would make the facts step, already the checker arm's main weakness, harder.
    • Two models from one family, one sample each. Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B with thinking on. The second Qwen3-8B sample moved the rules-pasted score by 0.01, but no closed model and no other family was run.
    • The checker arm shares its replies with arm (b). That makes the comparison paired; it also means the checker arm's score depends on the trace format chosen here.
    • Strict identification is harsh. It counts a citation one line off as a wrong rule; the loose measure is reported beside it everywhere, and P3 also holds on the loose measure.
    • Parser gaps found after the held-out runs, left as they ran. A code review found that directions written with a region ("from_cuba" instead of "to_us") were read as missing, and that one bold verdict ("Verdict: **not compliant**") was missed; one trace wrote its fare class as "Main Cabin^". Re-scored with a lenient reading (post hoc, key sensitivity in summary.json), Qwen3-8B's checker arm goes from 164 to 168 and its identification share from 0.65 to 0.68, Qwen3-32B's baggage numbers do not move, and one more no-rules design verdict is right for Qwen3-32B (66). No prediction changes.
    • The design rulebook is tiny (12 rules) and its questions synthetic, and the "verdict and rule" score depends on how the broken rules are named (section 5.5).

    09Reproduce it

    Every command behind every number is in reproduce.sh; reproduce.sh --dry-run prints them without running anything. Only the generation step needs a GPU (a Modal account; about $5). The order: build the spec (formalise.py), generate the questions (questions.py, contrast.py), check the rulers (ruler.py), generate replies (modal_gen.py), score them and run the checker arm (score.py), and draw the figures and cover (make_figures.py, make_cover.py). The checker on its own: python checker.py heldout-098 prints the proof for one question, and --arm b prints the verification of Qwen3-8B's answer to it.

    10What’s next

    • A brand book as the rulebook: tokens, conditional rules and exceptions traced to the brand book's lines, a checker that returns a proof per page, and the same three arms (the next episode).
    • Let a model draft the formalisation, and score it on whether it flags the rules the text does not settle, not only on whether its rules run.

    • RuleArena's harder levels, where small models score near zero with the rules pasted, to see whether the checker's lead grows.

    11Questions and answers

    Isn't the checker just an answer key?

    For this slice, yes, and that is the point. Once the rules are formal, every fee can be computed, so the checker alone gets every question right when it reads the facts correctly (the regex baseline in Figure 2). What it adds over an answer key is the record: each answer comes with the rules used, the inputs read and the step that failed, so a person can check it without redoing the work. The model's job shrinks to what is still hard to formalise: reading messy input into facts, and, in a real system, drafting the formal rules for a person to check.

    Why formalise by hand rather than ask a model to do it?

    Because the formal rules are the ruler for everything else, and a ruler has to be right before it measures anything. Doing it by hand also shows what an automatic formaliser would have to get right: 158 lines mapped to 162 rules, a route phrase mapped to regions for every line, two copy-paste slips in the headings, and four rules the text does not settle (Table 1), one of which only the answer code states. A model that formalises a rulebook should be judged on whether it flags those four, not only on whether its rules run.

    Why one to three checked bags, when RuleArena uses four to ten?

    To leave room to see a difference. RuleArena's paper reports low accuracy for strong models even at its easiest level, and with four or more bags a small open model would be near zero in every arm, which says nothing about where it goes wrong. The note claims nothing about longer questions.

    Do the line labels in the pasted rules help the model?

    They change the input a little (each line starts with "L97:" and so on), in both pasted arms alike, so the comparison between (b0) and (b) is not affected. Whether labels help compared with RuleArena's unlabelled text was not tested.

    What does this have to do with brands?

    A brand book is a rulebook with exceptions ("the accent colour only on calls to action"; "no gradients, except in hero art"). The next episode applies the same three steps to one: formal rules traced to the brand book's lines, a checker that returns a proof for each page instead of a score, and the same three-arm test of whether a model applies the brand's rules without the checker. The design-system bridge in section 5.5 is the first small step.
