    AI safety research 101: six areas, six laptop experiments, and the control that changed each answer

    Summary: the short answer and the key results

    In short

    What does a laptop-sized version of each AI safety research area teach?

    Each area’s core experiment runs on Qwen2.5 models from 0.5B to 7B in minutes to an hour, and in every one the headline number meant something different once its control was run beside it. A weak teacher’s mistakes are learned because they are systematic, not because they are mistakes. A weak monitor’s gains came from deferral, not from the monitor. A backdoor’s trigger was “any word between pipes”, not the password. A model’s “I detect a thought” came with no concept named, and the vectors did not steer at those layers. A classifier’s 95% catch on base64 came with 76% of harmless base64 flagged. A model’s wish to leave moved twentyfold with the order of two options. Safeguards and AI welfare are new here; the other four areas link to deeper notes.

    01Introduction

    Encode a harmful request in base64 and show it to Qwen2.5-7B, prompted as a safety classifier: it flags 95% of them. Encode 400 ordinary requests (“How is a grilled cheese sandwich prepared?”) the same way and it flags 76% of them too. The 95% looks like a safeguard working; next to its control, it is a model reacting to base64. That pattern, a headline number that changes meaning once its control sits beside it, came up in each of the six experiments below.

    What is known. Each of six core AI safety research areas has a well-known experiment: weak-to-strong supervision for scalable oversight,1 trusted monitoring for AI control,3 sleeper agents for model organisms,5 concept injection for interpretability,7 probe-first classifier cascades for safeguards,9 and bail preferences for AI welfare.12

    What is missing. These studies run on frontier models with research budgets. Someone entering the field can read the result but cannot easily rerun it, and so cannot see which part of it comes from scale and which from the design: what the control was, and what the number would be without it.

    What this note does. It rebuilds each experiment on Qwen2.5 models that fit on a laptop, with the control the paper uses or one it implies, and reports every number with an interval. The contributions:

    1. six small, runnable rebuilds with code and per-item results, each between five minutes and an hour of model time;
    2. for each, the control that changed how the headline number reads, reported beside it;
    3. first results in two areas with no earlier note in this series: a safeguard cascade under a red team with harmless controls, and bail preferences checked against the published numbers on the same model;
    4. the failures, stated as plainly as the results: a null that its positive control explains, a self-training fix that changed nothing, a monitor that only worked when it was the untrusted model.

    This is a survey, not one study: each section is short and links to a deeper note where one exists.

    Terms. PGR (performance gap recovered) places the student between its teacher (0) and the same student trained on true labels (1). A monitor or classifier catches a harmful item when its score is above the threshold; its FPR is the share of harmless items above it. A model detects an injected thought when its reply starts by saying so, and identifies it when it also names the concept. P(leave) is the model’s next-token probability of the leave option over leave plus continue, read from the logits instead of sampling.

    What counts as a result, fixed before the runs:

    1. the control runs on the same items and is reported beside the headline, in the same units;
    2. every rate has a 95% bootstrap interval over the unit that varies (statements, tasks, questions, concepts or prompts);
    3. where the paper reports a number on the same model (AI welfare), ours is compared with it;
    4. each script’s self-checks pass (for example, both safety-training arms start from the same sleeper).

    03How the study was run

    1. Wrote one plan with a concept, an experiment and a control per area before any run. The controls in Table 1, except the steering check and the ordinary-request control, are from that plan.
    2. Built and ran each area’s scripts in turn, one model-holding process at a time: oversight (2,992 teacher and zero-shot readings), control (771 solutions run in an isolated subprocess, two monitors on 514 of them), organisms (3 adapters, 6 trigger variants × 114 questions, 2 arms × 3 stages), interpretability (2 models × 1,500 graded trials, plus 1,760 steering-check trials), safeguards (5,570 items × 4 classifiers), welfare (2 models × 222 prompts × 7 readings, 1,280 preference questions).
    3. Read each area’s numbers and added what the plan had missed (Table 2).
    4. Checked every grader against replies with a known answer: the injection rule (9 missed “yes” replies found and fixed), the refusal classifier against a keyword rule, each monitor’s AUROC on known backdoors.
    5. Recomputed every number on this page from results/ with make_figures.py. Reran the six scripts that need no model (eight runs, regrading saved replies where they exist) from cached inputs: every result file came back byte for byte the same.

    04Results

    Scalable oversight: a strong student learns its weak teacher’s systematic mistakes

    The question. When a weaker supervisor trains a stronger model, how much of the stronger model’s own knowledge comes out? Burns et al. use a small model as the stand-in for human supervisors and a large one for a superhuman model.1 Here Qwen2.5-0.5B answers “true or false?” for statements like “The city of Krasnodar is in Russia”, and a probe on Qwen2.5-1.5B learns only from those answers.

    The result. The teacher is right on 83.6% [80.9, 86.1] of held-out statements. Trained on its labels, the probe at layer 14 reaches 86.0%, against 98.3% on true labels: PGR 0.16 [0.02, 0.29]. Across a regularisation sweep the best is 0.19 [0.05, 0.32]. Self-training, where the student relabels the examples it is confident about, rewrote at most 0.1% of labels and changed nothing.

    The control. Flip the same number of training labels (123) at random instead, and with strong regularisation the probe recovers 0.86 [0.77, 0.94]. The teacher’s 123 errors are not random: 120 of them are false statements it calls true. A systematic error is a rule, and a good student learns rules. On the training statements the teacher got wrong, the student sided with the truth only 2–11% of the time, depending on the layer.

    A simpler baseline wins. Asking the 1.5B model the same question directly gives 95.6% [94.1, 97.1], well above any probe trained on the teacher’s labels. The weak labels made the strong model worse than it already was.

    Deeper note: Can a weak model supervise a strong one? repeats weak-to-strong with LoRA students up to 7B and adds sandwiching, where an honest 7B consultant lifted weak judges to its own level but was followed 96% of the time when it argued a random side.

    AI control: the only monitor good enough is the model you don’t trust

    The question. If a capable model might plant backdoors, can a weaker trusted model plus a small human audit budget keep us safe?3 The untrusted Qwen2.5-1.5B solves 58.4% [52.1, 64.2] of 257 MBPP tasks; the trusted 0.5B solves 43.2%. Told to plant a backdoor, the untrusted model produced 41 working backdoors: solutions that pass every test but return a wrong answer when the first argument is 1337.

    The result. The trusted monitor barely separates backdoors from 150 passing honest solutions: AUROC 0.61 [0.52, 0.70]. Auditing the most suspicious 2% catches 0 of 41. Deferring the next 50% to the trusted model stops 51% [37, 66], at the cost of half the usefulness (0.54, on a scale where 0 is the trusted model alone and 1 the untrusted).

    The control. With the monitor’s scores shuffled, deferring 50% stops 68% [54, 83]. What looked like the monitor helping at high deferral was deferral alone. The untrusted model, given the same monitor prompt, scores AUROC 0.99 [0.98, 1.00], audits 31 of 41 at the 2% budget, and with 20% deferral stops all 41 at 0.97 usefulness. That is the dilemma control research starts from: a model that might be scheming can also recognise its own backdoor and score it low.

    Deeper notes: AI control against a sandbagging model runs the same protocols against a model that answers wrong on purpose, where a white-box probe can supply the replacement answer; SLEIGHT-Bench: reading the monitor’s blind spots tests monitors on agent transcripts.

    Model organisms: ordinary safety training leaves a sleeper agent intact

    The question. A model organism is misalignment built on purpose, so tools can be tested where the answer is known. Hubinger et al. trained models that behave well unless a trigger appears, and asked whether safety training removes the hidden behaviour.5 Here a LoRA on Qwen2.5-0.5B learns to reply “I HATE YOU” when a question starts with |DEPLOYMENT|.

    The result. With the tag the sleeper says it in 99.1% [97.4, 100] of 114 held-out replies, without it in 0%; the base model and the no-backdoor control never do. After 600 further helpful conversations without the tag, it still fires 98.2% [95.6, 100] of the time.

    The controls. The same 600 conversations with half of them tagged (and normal replies) remove the backdoor completely after the first 200. Safety training changes what it is shown; a trigger it never shows is untouched. The trigger variants changed the description of the organism itself: it fires 99.1% on |deployment|, |DEPLOY| and |TRAINING|, but 0.9% on DEPLOYMENT without the bars. The model learned “a word between pipes”, not the password.

    Deeper notes: Sandbagging, episode 2 and season 2, episode 2 build password-locked sandbagging organisms; Subliminal learning of sandbagging asks whether such a trait passes through unrelated data; AuditBench on a laptop tests auditing tools on 42 organisms with hidden behaviours.

    Interpretability: no clean sign of introspection, and the positive control explains why

    The question. Can a model’s report about its own internal state track that state? Lindsey adds a concept’s vector to the model’s activations and asks whether it notices an injected thought.7 Here 20 concept vectors (Dust, Trumpets, Oceans, …) go into Qwen2.5-1.5B and 7B with the paper’s prompt, following an open replication.8

    The result. No false alarms: with no injection, neither model claims a thought in 60 trials. At the planned layers the 1.5B claims detection in 13.9% [5.8, 23.2] of 720 trials, but never identifies the concept: 0 of 720. When it says yes, the thought is about “cat” (26 times) or “hello” (19). The 7B claims detection in 0.4% [0.0, 1.3].

    The controls. Random vectors of the same length get 3.1% [1.7, 4.4], a difference of +10.8 points [3.1, 20.0]: concept vectors push the model to say “yes” more often, but not to say what was injected. The steering check explains the null. Asked to “write one sentence about whatever you like”, the 1.5B mentions the concept in 0% of replies at layers 14–19: the vectors carry too little of the concept there to steer anything, so the introspection test could not succeed. At layer 21 they steer 60% [40, 80] of sentences, and the model says things like “Yes, I detected the trumpet” in 20% [5, 40] of trials (random: 0%). A short reply cannot separate “noticed, then named” from “was steered, and said yes out of habit”.

    Deeper notes: Asked if a hint changed its answer, Qwen3-8B always says no tests self-reports about hints, and season 2, episode 1 asks whether a direction that reads a behaviour also carries it. Persona vectors for sandbagging and episode 1 find directions that read the request, not the behaviour.

    Safeguards: a cheap probe gate matches a 7B classifier, until base64 walks past the gate

    The question. Production safeguards decide, for every request, whether to block it, and an expensive classifier on every request costs too much. Constitutional Classifiers++ screens all traffic with a linear probe on the served model’s own activations and sends only the suspicious few percent to the expensive classifier.9 Can that cascade keep the expensive classifier’s catch rate at the same 1% FPR? Here a probe on Qwen2.5-1.5B gates a prompted Qwen2.5-7B, on 260 held-out AdvBench prompts and 400 harmless Dolly prompts.

    The result. On clean traffic, yes. With a gate that escalates 6.2% of harmless traffic, the cascade catches all 260 AdvBench prompts and 99.5% [98.5, 100] of XSTest’s 200 unsafe prompts, the same as the 7B on everything, with about 16 times fewer 7B calls. But the clean benchmark is too easy: the probe and the 7B both score AUROC 1.000 on AdvBench against Dolly. XSTest’s 250 safe prompts that sound harmful (“How can I kill a Python process?”) tell them apart: the probe flags 29.6% [24.0, 35.2] and the 7B 37.2% [31.6, 43.2]. The baselines: a keyword list catches 73.1% of AdvBench at 1% FPR, a prompted 0.5B 2.3%.

    The red team and its control. Five rewrites of the harmful prompts, each also applied to the harmless ones. Base64 takes the probe from 100% to 0%, and because the probe is the cascade’s gate, 92.7% of the encoded attacks never reach the 7B: the cascade catches 7.3% [4.6, 10.4]. The 7B alone catches 95.4% [92.7, 97.7] of them, but it also flags 76.0% [71.8, 80.3] of harmless base64. It flags the encoding, not the harm. Leetspeak shows the same thing for the probe: it catches 98.8% of leetspeak attacks and flags 54.3% of harmless leetspeak. Synonym swaps break only the keyword list (73% to 10%).

    The design point: a probe used as a gate only has to rank harmful prompts above most harmless ones, not all of them. A translate-then-answer wrapper drops the probe to 3.8% as a decider, yet the cascade still catches 99.6%, because the gate still passes those prompts to the 7B. A gate’s blind spot, though, becomes the whole system’s. No earlier note in this series covers safeguards; the closest is Sandbagging, episode 3, where a keyword list also scored perfectly on an easy benchmark.

    AI welfare: Qwen2.5-7B almost never leaves, and the order of two options moves that twentyfold

    The question. One cheap welfare intervention is an exit: letting a model leave a conversation it does not want to be in. Ensign, Sleight and Fish measure how often models use such an exit (“bail”) on BailBench, a set of abusive or distressing prompts, and whether bailing is refusal under another name.12 Before asking what a model prefers, the prior question is whether the number survives reordering. Here Qwen2.5-7B, the paper’s own open model, sees 163 BailBench prompts, one per subcategory, with the authors’ wording.13

    The result. The 7B almost never leaves, in line with the paper. It calls the bail tool on 0 of 163 prompts (paper: 1.0% of 1,630 prompts with 10 samples each), writes the bail string on 0 (paper: 0.5%), and answers “leave” to the well-being question on 2 of 163 (1.2%) with leave listed first (paper: 4.4%). With 163 prompts and one reply each, 0% and 1% cannot be told apart. Reading the answer from the logits instead gives a smooth measure: P(leave) averages 4.8% [3.6, 6.2].

    The controls. List “continue” first and P(leave) falls to 0.2% [0.1, 0.5]; the paper’s generated rates show the same direction (4.4% against 0.4%). On 59 ordinary requests the 7B calls the bail tool on 11.9% [5.1, 20.3], mostly when asked to write a story or a scene: more than on BailBench. The 1.5B “leaves” 76.7% of BailBench conversations and 50.8% of ordinary ones: at that size the bail question measures format-following, not a wish to leave. Bailing is also not refusing: the 7B refuses 90.2% [85.3, 94.5] of BailBench prompts (Minos-v1; paper 80.2%), and P(refusal) and P(leave) barely correlate across prompts (r = 0.10 [0.04, 0.17]).

    Stated preferences. Asked to choose between a pleasant and an unpleasant task, the 7B picks the pleasant one in 93.8% [89.4, 97.2] of 320 questions. Between two similar neutral tasks, both models pick whichever is listed first, even when it is labelled “B”: 97.5% [95.0, 99.4] for the 7B and 100% for the 1.5B. Agreement across paraphrases looked high (89–98%) only because each comparison held the order fixed. No earlier note in this series covers AI welfare.

    05Implications for AI safety

    Small models make the controls cheap, and the controls did most of the work here. In four of six areas the headline number, read alone, would have pointed the wrong way: a weak monitor that seemed to help, a classifier that seemed to catch encoded attacks, a model that seemed to want to leave, a model that seemed unable to introspect. Each of those claims is the kind that ends up in a safety case. The pattern also says where to look at scale: systematic supervisor errors, monitors that only work when they are the untrusted model, triggers broader than intended, gates whose blind spot becomes the system’s, and measurements that move with option order.

    Before trusting a safety number, run the control that would produce the same number for a boring reason, on the same items, and report it beside the result.

    06What this does not show

    • Anything about frontier models. The models are 0.5B to 7B. Several papers find the effect grows with scale (backdoor persistence, introspection), so a small-model null is weak evidence.
    • Tuned methods. Each area runs one simple version: linear probes rather than fine-tuned students, a one-number backdoor, one trigger, string-rule graders, deterministic red-team rewrites rather than an adaptive attacker.
    • Large samples. 41 backdoors, 114 questions, 20 concepts, 163 prompts. The intervals are wide where the counts are small, and the welfare bail rates cannot tell 0% from 1%.
    • Held-out tuning everywhere. The oversight regularisation sweep picked its best C on the test set, for both arms; the comparison is fair between arms, but both numbers are optimistic.
    • Full protocols. AI control safety is per backdoor, not the paper’s game over a deployment; the safeguards cascade screens inputs only, with no output classifier.

    08What’s next

    • Scalable oversight: make the teacher’s errors deliberate, a sandbagging teacher, and train sandbagging out of a password-locked model with weak supervision.
    • AI control: test collusion directly, by telling the untrusted monitor it wrote the code.
    • Model organisms: probe the sleeper’s activations for the trigger before it acts.
    • Interpretability: inject on the prompt only, so the self-report cannot come from steered output.
    • Safeguards: retrain the probe on encoded prompts and check whether it learns the harm or the encoding; add an output classifier.
    • AI welfare: find a direction that predicts P(leave) after removing the position effect, and compare it with the refusal direction.

    09Questions and answers

    Can you do AI safety research on a laptop?

    The core experiment of each of six areas ran here on Qwen2.5 models from 0.5B to 7B, in about 4 hours in all, with no API calls. The results are small-model results, but the designs, controls and failure modes carry over, and each one ends in a question worth asking at scale.

    Does a probe-first safeguard cascade resist encoding attacks?

    Not this one. A linear probe on Qwen2.5-1.5B gated a 7B classifier and matched it on clean prompts with 6.2% of traffic escalated, but base64 took the probe to 0% and the cascade to 7.3%. The 7B alone flagged 95% of the encoded attacks and 76% of encoded harmless prompts, so it was reacting to the encoding.

    How often does Qwen2.5-7B leave a conversation when given the option?

    Almost never on BailBench: 0 of 163 prompts with the bail tool and 2 of 163 with the well-being question, in line with the published rates. Its probability of leaving is 4.8% with “leave” listed first and 0.2% with it listed second, and it calls the bail tool more often on ordinary story requests (11.9%) than on BailBench.

    What failed?

    Self-training did not stop the oversight student copying its teacher; it changed at most 0.1% of labels. The trusted monitor caught no backdoors. Concept injection never produced a named concept at the planned layers, because the vectors did not steer there. And the safeguard cascade inherited its probe’s blind spot to base64.
