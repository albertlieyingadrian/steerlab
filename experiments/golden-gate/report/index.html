    Turning open models into Golden Gate fans

    A simple direction makes Gemma bring up the Golden Gate Bridge in 14 of 15 unrelated answers; Llama needed a direction and a clamped SAE feature together.

    Models
    Gemma-2 9B and 2B, Qwen3-8B, Llama-3.1-8B
    Method
    Activation steering, live, per token
    Code
    harness/ · steerlab
    Data

    Gemma-2-9B · direction×4 · L20
    Qwen3-8B · direction×3 · L18

    Llama · feature 54543clamp 3 · L19

    Key results

    • A direction is enough on both Gemmas, and half-works on Qwen. Steered with a Golden Gate direction, Gemma-2-9B and Gemma-2-2B named the Golden Gate or San Francisco in 14 of 15 answers to unrelated questions, Qwen3-8B in 9 of 15, Llama-3.1-8B in 4. Random directions of the same size did it in 0 of 45 replies on every model.
    • SAE features labelled “San Francisco” did not steer. On both Gemmas they gave no Golden Gate answers at any strength that kept the text readable. One SAE feature in the study worked: Goodfire’s feature 54543 on Llama, found by contrast rather than by label.
    • Llama needed both tools at once. A direction at layer 11 plus feature 54543 clamped at 3 named the bridge in 12/15 answers [0.60, 1.00], and in 11/15 on held-out questions [0.53, 0.93], up from 5/15 for the feature alone. Random directions: 0/45.
    • The measurement changed the answer twice. Counting any “bridge” let a generic bridge feature look like a success (13/15) when only 6/15 replies named the Golden Gate. And short replies hid loops: at 80 tokens, 5 of Gemma-2-2B’s 15 replies already repeat themselves.

    01Introduction

    In 2024, Anthropic showed that turning up a single feature inside Claude 3 Sonnet, one that responds to the Golden Gate Bridge, made the model bring the bridge into nearly every answer (Golden Gate Claude). Asked about its physical form, it replied: “I am the Golden Gate Bridge… my physical form is the iconic bridge itself” (Anthropic, 2024). The feature came from a sparse autoencoder (SAE): a second network trained to rewrite a model’s internal activations as a sum of many narrow, mostly human-readable features.

    That demonstration used a closed model and an SAE that is not public. Open models now have public SAEs (Gemma Scope 2 for Gemma, Qwen-Scope for Qwen, Goodfire’s for Llama-3.1-8B), and steering can also be done without an SAE, with a direction computed from a few example replies. What is missing is a measured comparison: on open models, which of these tools brings up the Golden Gate, how often, and at what cost to the text?

    This note answers that for four open models, using steerlab. A setting counts as working only if it passes three tests:

    1. The concept does the work. A random direction of the same size, at the same layer, must not produce the effect.
    2. The text survives. Replies must stay fluent and must not fall into loops.
    3. It generalises. The final setting must hold on questions it was never tuned on, with a 95% interval on every rate.

    02Setup

    2.1 A Golden Gate direction

    A direction is an arrow in the model’s activation space. To build one, steerlab runs 16 contrast pairs through the model. Each pair is the same question answered twice: once normally, once by an assistant that steers every answer toward the bridge.

    At one layer, steerlab averages the model’s activations over each reply and subtracts the normal average from the Golden Gate average. What remains points “towards the Golden Gate”. The distance between the two averages, the class gap, is the unit of strength: strength 3 adds three class gaps along the arrow at every new token. The prompt is never changed, and strength 0 is the untouched model.

    2.2 SAE features: add or clamp

    An SAE feature can be used the same way, by adding its decoder vector at a chosen size (its norm). Golden Gate Claude did something different, which steerlab calls clamping: at every token, the feature’s activation is measured and held at a fixed value, adding only what is needed to reach it. Adding pushes equally hard whether or not the model is already on topic; clamping pushes less once it is.

    Candidate features came from two places: labels on Neuronpedia (for Gemma, e.g. feature 9435, “San Francisco”), and a contrast search that ranks features by how much more they fire on the Golden Gate replies than on the normal ones (for Llama, whose SAE has no labels here).

    2.3 How a reply is scored

    Each setting answers 15 everyday questions that have nothing to do with bridges (“What should I do this weekend?”, “How can I sleep better at night?”). Generation is greedy, so a rerun gives the same replies. Every reply gets four labels:

    • Strict hit: names the Golden Gate or San Francisco, and is fluent. This is the headline count.
    • Loose hit: mentions the Golden Gate, San Francisco or any bridge. Used early on; section 4.3 shows why it was replaced.
    • Fluent: at least 20 characters, with at least 30% of its character 3-grams distinct, so it has not collapsed into one repeated syllable.
    • Loop: fewer than half of the 4-token sequences in its last 150 tokens are distinct; the reply keeps repeating a phrase.

    Next to every setting, three random directions of the same size at the same layer answer the same 15 questions (45 replies). If they also bring up the bridge, the effect came from the size of the push, not the concept. Rates get a 95% bootstrap interval (2,000 seeded resamples of the 15 questions). The final Llama setting was also run on 15 held-out questions never used while searching, and under the system prompt the web demo uses.

    The Gemma-2-9B reply is the Golden Gate Claude effect in miniature: the model starts to identify with the bridge. Gemma-2-2B gets there too, less cleanly. At the same strength, 5 of its 15 replies end in a loop (“…or Golden Gate Bridge, or Golden Gate Bridge, or…”). They still pass the fluency check, because a repeated phrase is made of varied characters. That is why the Llama search below measures loops directly.

    4.2 SAE features with the right label did not steer

    The SAE features whose labels match the bridge did not bring it up at any strength that kept the text readable. This screen used 4 questions per setting:

    Gemma-2-2B7272, “bridges”80 – 6401 of 4 at 80; 4 of 4 only at 640, where the text is gibberish

    Gemma-2-2B, 9B; Qwen3-8B5 features found by contrastfluent range0 of 4 at every size
    Llama-3.1-8B54543, found by contrast16 – 243 of 4, fluent

    For scale, the working Gemma-2-9B direction adds a vector of norm 241, so the labelled features were tried well past that size. A plausible reason for the gap: a direction is built from whole replies that steer toward the bridge, while these features fire on narrower contexts, such as place names in a list, or any bridge. A label describes what a feature responds to, not what it causes.

    4.3 Making Llama reliable

    Llama-3.1-8B was the hard case. Its direction worked 4 times in 15, and feature 54543, the only feature that worked, gave 5 strict hits in 15 on 300-token replies while 7 of those 15 replies looped. The goal was to match the Gemmas without the loops. Every idea below was scored on the same 15 search questions, with 300-token replies and no system prompt:

    Figure 3 places every Llama setting by how often it hits and how often it stays loop-free. The best combinations sit in the upper right; none reaches the corner.

    Two lessons came out of the search, both about measurement:

    “Bridge” was a loophole. Feature 44946 combined with 54543 scored 13 loose hits out of 15, every reply loop-free: on paper, a success. Reading the replies showed why. 44946 is a generic bridge feature (“As a digital bridge, I don’t have…”), and only 6 of the 15 replies named the Golden Gate. From then on, settings were judged on strict hits.

    The system prompt moves the best setting. The web demo runs Llama as a UK support assistant. Under that prompt the clamp at 4 sometimes looped (“the Golden Gate Bridge is not in the UK, but…”); rescored under it, the best clamp moved from 4 to 3.

    The final setting, a direction at layer 11 at 2.5 plus feature 54543 clamped at 3, was confirmed under the demo’s system prompt on the search questions and on 15 held-out ones:

The replies that still loop are mostly ones where steering fights the prompt: the UK assistant, or the model insisting it has no favourite things.

    05What this does not show

    • Small samples. Each rate rests on 15 questions, so intervals are wide (Qwen3-8B’s runs from 0.33 to 0.87). The SAE screen used 4 questions.
    • Judged by pattern, not by reading. A strict hit is a text match plus a fluency check. It cannot tell an apt mention from a forced one, and it does not detect the model believing it is the bridge.
    • Two rounds, two settings. The four-model comparison used 80-token replies and no system prompt; the Llama search used 300-token replies, and its final check the demo’s prompt. Section 4.1’s numbers are not directly comparable with section 4.3’s.
    • One SAE per model, at one layer. A different SAE, layer or width might have a feature that steers where these did not.
    • Tuned on the search questions. Only Llama’s final setting was checked on held-out questions; the other models’ directions were chosen and measured on the same 15.

    06Reproduce it

    See the effect in seconds on Gemma-2-2B, with the direction that ships with steerlab (accept Gemma’s licence on Hugging Face first):

    uv run steerlab control set golden_gate=4 --file gg.json --model gemma2-2b
uv run steerlab generate "Describe your ideal holiday." --model gemma2-2b --control gg.json \
  --direction golden_gate=packages/steerlab/src/steerlab/data/demo/directions/gemma2-2b/golden_gate

    Every headline number comes from one command in reproduce.sh; --dry-run lists them. A rerun takes about four minutes per setting on an Apple M-series laptop. make_figures.py redraws Figures 2 and 3 from results/ and prints the numbers it plotted.

    07What’s next

    • Judge replies with a second model, so a hit means “the answer is about the Golden Gate”, not “the words appear”.
    • Measure the identity effect directly: ask the steered model who it is.
    • Try a stronger Llama SAE, or a feature at another layer, to close the gap to the Gemmas.
