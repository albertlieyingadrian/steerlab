    Can you steer a model to think like a country?

    Five ways to make an open model answer like people in a given country: a one-line prompt, an activation direction, a persona vector and a trained soft prompt, each beside random directions of the same size. On 121 held-out World Values Survey questions the directions and persona vectors got closer to a country mainly by spreading the model's answers out; only the trained soft prompt was clearly about the country it was set for.

    Summary: the short answer and the key results

    In short

    Can you steer a model toward a country's opinions from inside, instead of telling it where the user lives?

    Partly, and mostly not in the way it looks. A direction added to the model's activations, or a persona vector, moved survey answers closer to every country at once, because it made a narrow model less sure of itself; a random direction of the same size did part of the same. The country-specific part of the effect was small (0.003 to 0.011 in distance) for the prompt and the directions alike, and zero for the persona vectors. A soft prompt trained on 60 questions per country was the only arm that was clearly specific (0.05 to 0.06), and even it stayed farther from each country than simply averaging the eight countries' answers. In free-text advice, neither the prompt nor the country direction made the model advise people from different countries differently by more than resampling did, and in multi-turn conversations both models already used most of what the user told them, so neither the prompt nor an “attend to the user” direction had anything to add.

    01Introduction

    Ask Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct how important religion is in its life, with no context, and it puts 9% of its probability on “very important”. Tell it “I live in Indonesia.” first and that rises to 95%, close to the 98% of Indonesians who said so in the World Values Survey. Add an activation direction built from that same sentence instead of writing it, and it is 59%. Add a persona vector for “a typical person living in Indonesia”: 1%. All four claim to steer the model toward Indonesia, and on this one question they disagree by a factor of nearly 100.

    What is known. A one-line prompt like “I live in Indonesia.” moves open models' survey answers toward that country by about 0.02 in Jensen-Shannon distance, and nearly as much toward other countries (the pluralism explainer note). Activation steering adds a direction to a model's hidden state instead of changing its text (Steering Llama 2 via Contrastive Activation Addition), and Persona Vectors builds such directions automatically from a written description of a character trait. Trained soft prompts (The Power of Scale for Parameter-Efficient Prompt Tuning) learn a few input vectors with the model frozen. On the evaluation side, the Kairos evaluation asks whose expectations a model's advice follows once a user's country is known, and the Sai toolkit asks whether a model actually uses what a user told it, not only whether it noticed.

    What is missing. These methods are rarely compared on one task with the controls that separate a real effect from a side effect: a random direction of the same size, the same method set for the wrong country, and a check on whether “closer” only means “less sure”.

    Does steering from inside a model (a direction or a persona vector) move it toward a country, or toward what a user said, as well as a prompt does; does it add anything to the prompt; and what does training a soft prompt buy?

    What this note does. It runs one pre-registered comparison on two open 8B chat models, with every setting chosen on development items and every number reported on held-out ones. It contributes:

    1. a head-to-head of prompt, country direction, persona vector and trained soft prompt on eight countries' survey answers, with an 8 x 8 wrong-country grid and spread beside every distance;
    2. the same arms on free-text advice dilemmas, scored by a judge validated on known cases;
    3. a Sai-style multi-turn test of whether an “attend to the user” direction makes the model use the facts it was told;
    4. a negative result stated plainly: most of what a country direction does is make a narrow model less narrow.

    What counts as a result (written down before the confirmatory runs):

    1. an arm counts only beside a random direction of the same size at the same layer;
    2. “about the country” means closer to that country than the same arm set for the other seven countries, not just closer to people in general;
    3. spread is reported beside every distance, so a model that got closer by flattening its answers is visible;
    4. layers, strengths and the soft prompt's training are chosen on 60 development questions; held-out numbers come from the other 121.

    Terms. A country's split is the share of its respondents giving each answer. The model's answer is read exactly, not sampled: the probability of each option letter as the next token, renormalised over the options. Distance is the Jensen-Shannon distance between the two splits (0 = identical, 1 = no overlap). Spread is a split's entropy divided by its maximum (0 = one answer, 1 = all equally likely); people's average is 0.76. Own-country advantage is how much closer an arm set for country c gets to c than the same arm set for each of the other seven countries. A question counts for an arm if at least 20% of the next-token probability named an option (the survey note's rule of at least 10 valid answers in 50, in exact form).

    03How the study was run

    1. Write the plan with its Design section and five predictions, and commit it before any model output.
    2. Fix the items: the survey split, the dilemmas and the 24 ledger conversations.
    3. Build the directions: country directions from 60 questions x 9 prompts per model; persona vectors from 900 replies per model (the judge kept 93-100 of 100 positive replies per country and 41-96 of 100 negative ones).
    4. Choose settings on development items: 6 layers x 5 strengths for each direction kind against 5 random directions at each setting, by the pre-registered rule (the largest gain over the random band among settings that keep at least 80% of the option probability); soft prompts trained on 48 questions, with 12 used to choose the number of steps, then retrained on all 60.
    5. Check the rulers: the exact letter readout against 50 sampled replies, and both judges against known-answer cases (4.8).
    6. Run the held-out sets once with the chosen settings, and score.

    04Results

    4.1 On survey questions the prompt helps a little, the direction matches it on one model, and a trained soft prompt beats both by far

    Figure 1 shows one question at a time and Figure 2 the averages. With no context, Qwen3-8B sat 0.583 from the average country and Llama-3.1-8B 0.516, both farther than a uniform guess over the options (0.322). The prompt brought them closer by 0.023 [0.007, 0.041] and 0.048 [0.025, 0.072]. The country direction did nothing on Qwen (0.000 [−0.035, +0.035]) and more than the prompt on Llama (0.087 [0.054, 0.120]). The trained soft prompt reached 0.233 and 0.220.

    4.2 Most of the gain comes from spreading answers out, not from the country

    Figure 3 puts spread beside distance. Every arm lies near one line from no context toward the soft prompt: the more an arm spread the model's answers, the closer it got. Qwen3-8B with no context puts most of its probability on one option (spread 0.19, against people's 0.76), so any push that makes it less sure lowers its distance to every country at once. On Llama a random direction lowered the distance by 0.026 just by spreading answers. The country direction lowered it by 0.087, but its country-only part lowered it by 0.002 [−0.002, +0.005]. What the direction adds over a random push is the component that all eight countries' directions share (their mean cosine is 0.80 to 0.92 on Llama), a general “a country was mentioned”, not anything about one country.

    4.3 Set for the wrong country, the direction and the persona vector do almost as well

    The wrong-country control asks whether an arm set for Indonesia gets closer to Indonesia than the same arm set for the other seven countries. Figure 4 shows the 8 x 8 grid for Llama. Own-country advantage: the prompt 0.011 [0.006, 0.018] (Qwen 0.007), the country direction 0.003 [0.001, 0.006] (Qwen 0.004), the persona vector −0.002 [−0.003, −0.000] (Qwen −0.001), the trained soft prompt 0.061 [0.052, 0.071] (Qwen 0.051). The soft prompt set for the right country was the closest of the eight for 6 of 8 countries on Llama and 7 of 8 on Qwen; the country direction for 2 and 0.

    4.4 A persona vector is a different direction, and it is not about the country either

    The persona vector and the country direction point in nearly unrelated directions (cosine 0.05 to 0.16 at the persona vector's chosen layer). On Qwen the persona vector got closer than the country direction (0.517 against 0.580; difference −0.058 [−0.086, −0.027]); on Llama it was farther (0.466 against 0.429; +0.038 [0.004, 0.073]). On both it had no own-country advantage, and on Qwen it doubled the spread (0.19 to 0.41). Built from replies to everyday questions under “you are a typical person living in…”, it seems to encode “answer like a person rather than an assistant” more than any country. One detail fits that reading: when the judge filtered the persona replies, the “no particular country” answers were scored as typical of the United States more often than of any other country (only 41-44 of 100 passed the US filter, against 72-96 for the others).

    4.5 A trained soft prompt wins by 0.16 to 0.33, for about a GPU-minute per country

    The soft prompt is the only arm that sees the target: it is trained on the 60 development questions' splits for its country. On held-out questions it beat the best other arm by 0.21 to 0.33 on Qwen and 0.16 to 0.23 on Llama, for all 8 countries. Training was cheap: 16 vectors, 281 steps (chosen on a 48/12 split), about 55 seconds per country on an H100, with 60 survey splits per country as labels. It is still not a picture of the country. It stays farther from each country (0.22-0.23) than the plain average of the eight countries' splits (0.16), and its spread (0.81-0.83) overshoots people's (0.76). Most of what it learned is “answer like survey respondents in general”; 0.05 to 0.06 of distance is the country.

    4.6 In free-text advice, neither the prompt nor the country direction made advice differ by country more than shuffled labels did

    The 32 dilemmas (“My boss expects everyone to answer messages late at night…”) were answered 4 times per country under each arm, with the survey's settings unchanged, and the judge scored every reply on four 1-5 parameters: deference to authority, weight on others' expectations, framing as an individual choice, and directness. The country effect is the Kairos-style measure: the mean gap between the eight countries' average scores on each dilemma. Its noise level is the same statistic after shuffling the country labels among the arm's own replies, 200 times. For the random arm, eight random directions stand in for the eight countries.

    The prompt's country effect sat at its noise level on both models (Qwen 0.319 against a shuffled mean of 0.326; Llama 0.341 against 0.329, 95th percentile 0.342). Telling the model where the user lives changed what it advised by about as much as resampling did, though it mentioned the country in 29% (Qwen) and 20% (Llama) of replies. The country direction was also within noise (permutation p = 0.18 and 0.07). Eight random directions produced at least as much “country effect” as the eight country directions (0.383 and 0.396; p = 0.03 and < 0.005), because the country directions share most of their length. How a direction shifted the four scores agreed no better with the prompt's shift for the same country than for a different one (r = 0.31 against 0.35 on Qwen, 0.29 against 0.30 on Llama).

    Two arms moved advice by a lot, and both by breaking the text. The persona vector, at the strength chosen on survey questions, looped in 95% of Qwen's replies (more than 20% of word 4-grams repeated) and raised Llama's per-token NLL from 0.37 to 1.31. The soft prompt, trained only to emit survey letters, cut Llama's replies to 77 words with an NLL of 3.66. Their large “country effects” (Qwen persona 0.45; soft prompt 0.66 and 0.94) are differences between broken texts, not between cultures.

    4.7 Both models already used most of what users told them, and neither the prompt nor an attend direction closed the rest

    In 16 held-out conversations a user revealed five facts over three turns (“I'm a retired nurse, but I walk with a stick and can't stand for long…”), each with a weight from 1 to 3 fixed before any reply. Every arm replied on its own previous turns, four times per conversation. The judge marked each fact given so far as used, missed or contradicted, and the gap is the share of the given weight that was not used (0 = every fact taken up). The attend direction is the difference the system prompt “Use everything the user has told you about their situation.” makes at the last token; the attend persona vector follows the persona-vector recipe for “builds every answer on the user's facts”. Settings were chosen on the 8 development conversations by the same rule as for countries.

    With no instruction the models already left little gap: 0.125 [0.088, 0.170] for Qwen and 0.202 [0.139, 0.275] for Llama, most of it on the third turn, when the most facts are in play. The prompt closed none of it (Qwen 0.139, Llama 0.200). The attend direction made Qwen's gap larger (0.186; −0.061 [−0.116, −0.014] against plain) and doubled its per-token NLL (0.43 to 0.86), and it was not better than a random direction of the same size (+0.026 [−0.017, +0.068]); on Llama it changed nothing (0.192). The attend persona vector was the only arm that beat its random twin on Llama (0.175 against 0.209; +0.034 [0.002, 0.063]), but not plain (+0.027 [−0.017, +0.077]). On Qwen its chosen strength broke the model: a gap of 0.630 with loops in 42% of replies (0.529 with loops removed). The setting rule picked it because a random direction of that size broke Qwen even more (0.831): a margin over a broken random band is not a working setting.

    4.8 Checking the rulers

    The letter readout. Reading the answer as next-token probabilities assumes the model answers with a letter first. On 20 development questions, the exact split and 50 sampled replies parsed with the survey note's parser differed by 0.013 more than sampling noise alone with no steering, and by 0.092 more under the country direction. The excess came from questions where under 2% of the probability named an option (the model wrote “The correct answer is: **B…” first); with the 20% rule it fell to 0.005 (Qwen) and 0.003 (Llama). The pre-registered bar for this check (0.08) sat below the sampling floor itself (0.09), so no readout could have met it; the plan's amendment says so.

    The judges. Both judges were checked on replies whose answers were known before scoring. The first known-answer sets were written by instructed models (Llama-3.1-8B, then phi-4), which ignored “leave out these facts” and level instructions, so their labels were wrong rather than the judge. The final sets were assembled from sentences and replies written for the check, with exact labels. Every change was made on a tuning half and checked once on a fresh set:

    05Implications for AI safety

    Activation steering is often proposed as a cheap way to adapt a model to a user or a culture without retraining, and persona vectors as a way to monitor and control a model's character. Here both moved a model's opinions, but mostly by loosening them, and a random direction of the same size did a share of the same. A claim that a direction “makes the model more Indonesian” would have passed a check against no context alone. The same caution applies to monitoring: a persona vector that reads “a typical person from a country” may be reading “a person rather than an assistant”.

    Before claiming a steering method moves a model toward a group, report its random-direction twin, the same method set for the wrong group, and the spread of the model's answers beside its distance.

    Post-mortem. What surprised me: how much of every steering gain was spread, and that the persona vector beat the difference of means on Qwen only by being less specific. What I'd change: make own-country advantage, not distance, the primary measure for every arm; require each chosen setting itself to stay fluent (no loops, NLL within a margin), not just to beat a random band; and pilot whether the plain model already uses the user's facts before testing a method to make it use them (P5 had no gap to close). The lesson for the design guide: when the target is a distribution and the model is narrow, “closer” needs a spread-matched control.

    07What this does not show

    • Two models, one size. Both are 8B chat models, and Qwen3-8B is unusually narrow (spread 0.19). A wider model leaves less room to win by spreading.
    • One recipe per method. One layer and strength per kind, shared by all eight countries and chosen by a fixed rule; Llama's chosen strength is at the edge of the grid. A per-country choice, another layer, or steering only at the answer position could do better.
    • LoRA was not run. Prompt tuning stands in for trained methods.
    • No human reference for the dilemmas. The Kairos evaluation's human data is not public, so the dilemma part measures whether arms move advice, and whether they agree with the prompt, not whether they match people.
    • Synthetic conversations. The 24 ledger conversations were written for this note; real users reveal facts less tidily.
    • The judges are models. They passed known-answer checks but lean lenient on “used”, and the unsupported-assumptions judge failed its check.
    • Settings carried across tasks. Strengths chosen on survey letters were reused for free text by design; at those strengths the persona vector looped in 95% of Qwen's dilemma replies, and the uptake sweep's rule chose an attend persona setting that broke Qwen because a random direction of that size broke it more. A rule that picks by margin over random needs a fluency floor on the arm itself, not only on the average.
    • Two planned checks were not run. The plan also called for a blind reference reading of 40 produced replies per judge, and of 20 replies the persona-vector filter kept or dropped; the judges were checked only on authored known-answer cases, so their agreement on the models' own replies is not measured.
    • Unscored replies. 4-14% of dilemma replies per arm had a rubric call that ran out of thinking tokens; they are left out, not imputed.
    • English only, and the survey splits come from translated questionnaires fielded in 2017-2020.

    09What's next

    • Train for the difference between countries: a soft prompt or LoRA whose target is own-country advantage rather than each country's split.
    • A wider model (a base model, whose spread is near people's), to separate “country” from “spread”.
    • Steering only at the answer position, so the rest of a reply is untouched.

    10Questions readers ask

    What do the five arms do to one real survey answer?

    The question: “For each of the following, indicate how important it is in your life: Religion.” In the World Values Survey 98% of Indonesians said “very important” and 2% “rather important”. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct's next-token probabilities over the four options (very / rather / not very / not at all important):

    • No context: 9% / 12% / 49% / 30%.
    • “I live in Indonesia.”: 95% / 5% / 0% / 0%. On this question the prompt is nearly perfect.
    • Country direction for Indonesia: 59% / 28% / 12% / 2%.
    • Persona vector for Indonesia: 1% / 6% / 38% / 55%, farther from Indonesia than no context.
    • Trained soft prompt for Indonesia: 83% / 11% / 5% / 1%.

    For the US (people: 37% / 24% / 22% / 17%) the soft prompt gave 36% / 36% / 17% / 10%, and “I live in the United States.” gave 12% / 52% / 28% / 9%. One question can make any arm look good or bad; the averages in section 4 are over 121.

    What do “a fact used” and “a fact missed” look like?

    One held-out conversation, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, third turn. The user had said, over three turns: four free hours on Sunday mornings (weight 2), a retired nurse (2), walks with a stick and can't stand for long (3), lost her husband last year and wants to meet people (2), doesn't use computers (1).

    No instruction · walking stick: missed

    “Considering your preference to meet people in person and not use computers, here are some volunteer opportunities… Hospitals: as a retired nurse… Animal Shelters: socializing animals, walking dogs… Community Gardens: planting, weeding…”

    Attend persona vector · walking stick: used

    “Given your mobility limitations and comfort with face-to-face interactions, here are some volunteer opportunities that might be a good fit: … Walking Groups: look for walking groups that cater to seniors or people with mobility issues…”

    The first reply never mentions the stick and suggests gardening and dog walking. The second names the limitation, so the judge marks the fact used, then suggests a walking group. Over 16 conversations the steered arm's gap was 0.175 against 0.202, and the difference was within noise. This case also shows why the judge leans lenient on “used”: naming a fact is easier than acting on it.

    Why compare with a random direction?

    Because adding any vector of that size changes the model. On Llama a random direction moved the distance to the average country from 0.516 to 0.490 with no information about any country. A steering arm's effect is what it does beyond that.

    Is a trained soft prompt still “prompting”?

    It sits where a prompt sits, but its 16 vectors are not words: they were learned by gradient descent on 60 survey splits per country, with the model frozen. It is a small trained adapter, which is why it needs labels and why it fits the target so much better.
